\sec Lightning duration

The duration of the lightning event was determined by identifying local maxima of illumination on the camera chip over time from peaks that had a prominence higher than twice the noise level. The first and last maxima were used to identify the first and last frames, and the lightning duration was calculated from the framerate. The first ten frames of the camera recording were omitted to avoid potential artifacts from video data compression. The noise amplitude was established from the first 100 frames (from frame 10 to frame 110). Examples of duration determination for some lightning events are shown in Figure \ref[Camera_lightning_duration]. The histogram of lightning event durations, shown in Figure \ref[duration_histogram], includes 107 lightning events measured during 12 thunderstorms.

To calculate the total length of a lightning event from a radio signal (example data in Fig.~\ref[UHF_lightning_duration] or \ref[figur_VLF_lightning_signals]), a similar methodology was used. A signal greater than $4\sigma$, calculated from a moving window of 10~ms, marks the beginning of the lightning event. This algorithm is applied symmetrically from both ends of the recorded signal. For detecting the lightning start, the process begins from the first sample of the signal, and for detecting the lightning end, the process begins from the last sample of the recorded signal.

The measured durations are usually in the order of hundreds of milliseconds; only exceptionally are there shorter lightning events. The median duration of lightning events is 524 ms. This result is significantly higher than was mentioned in previous studies, 200-300~ms \cite[rakov_uman_2003] and 350~ms  \cite[Lopez_Pineda_Montanya_Velde_Fabro_Romero_2017].

The processing of lightning recordings differs for day and night storm observations as they require different settings of high-speed cameras, in particular, the setting of exposure length and analog gain varies. This difference could affect the exact measurements of the total length of a discharge, resulting, however, only in a shortening of the estimated time duration. Because of sunlight scattered in the atmosphere during the daytime, the weak discharges might have been omitted. Therefore, the extracted time durations of lightning events could possibly be underestimated.

From the subsequent analysis of the lightning duration, two thunderstorms were excluded where it was not possible to distinguish individual lightning events, meaning there was no delay of at least 500 ms between the individual detected discharges. Lightning is considered to be an event where the time between the individual discharges does not exceed 100 ms.

The measured distribution of lightning flash durations was fitted with a log-normal probability density function (PDF), under the constraint that the median flash duration is fixed to the determined median of 524~ms. The resulting PDF is shown in Fig.~\ref[PDF_lightning]. The $\hbox{\rm PDF}(t)$ describes the probability density of observing a flash with duration~$t$, and satisfies
$$
\hbox{\rm PDF}(t) \ge 0
\quad\hbox{and}\quad
\int_{0}^{\infty} \hbox{\rm PDF}(t)\,\hbox{\rm d}t = 1 \, .
\eqmark
$$

\medskip
\clabel[PDF_lightning]{Probability density of lightning duration}
\picw=15cm \cinspic ./img/PDF_lightning.png
\caption/f   Probability density function (PDF) of observed lightning event durations. The histogram shows measured lightning durations from high-speed camera recordings, and the red curve shows a log-normal fit constrained by the measured median duration (524~ms).
\medskip

From the fitted PDF we obtain the cumulative distribution function (CDF), shown in Fig.~\ref[CDF_lightning]. The $\hbox{\rm CDF}(T)$ is defined as the probability that the lightning duration~$t$ does not exceed a given acquisition window~$T$:
$$
\hbox{\rm CDF}(T) = P(t \le T) = \int_{0}^{T} \hbox{\rm PDF}(t)\,\hbox{\rm d}t \, .
\eqmark
$$
Here, $P(\cdot)$ denotes the probability of the enclosed event.
Operationally, $\hbox{\rm CDF}(T)$ gives the fraction of lightning flashes that would be fully captured for any chosen maximum recording length~$T$.

\medskip
\clabel[CDF_lightning]{Cumulative distribution of lightning duration}
\picw=15cm \cinspic ./img/CDF_lightning.png
\caption/f   Cumulative distribution function (CDF) of lightning event durations derived from the fitted log-normal model. The dashed lines indicate the maximum continuous acquisition window of the used SDR receivers and the corresponding cumulative probability.
\medskip

In our apparatus, the radio receivers limit the continuous record length to $T = 1.45$~s. Evaluating the $\hbox{\rm CDF}$ at this value gives
$$
\hbox{\rm CDF}(1.45~\hbox{\rm s}) = P(t \le 1.45~\hbox{\rm s}) \;\approx\; 89.9\% \, ,
\eqmark
$$
which means the described instrumentation is theoretically capable of capturing the complete temporal evolution of almost~90\% of all observed lightning events. But at the same time, the value assumes an ideal fit of the lighting events in the recorded time window, which is not real for most cases.

\secc[VLF_LMA] Segment-based visualization for VLF lightning mapping

The described findings have direct implications, for example, on the way lightning discharge events should be displayed and localized. Given the typical dimensions and time scales on which discharges occur, it follows that even receivers operating on the VLF principle can provide meaningful descriptions of lightning discharge structures. At the same time, for the same reason, it is misleading to display lightning discharges on maps as points, which are intuitively interpreted as CG lightning strikes hitting the ground at approximately the position of the map mark. In reality, most of those points correspond to CC lightning discharges, given the actual incidence of their occurrence over CG lightning.

A better representation that considers this fact is attempted in Fig~\ref[VLF_lightning_map], which shows a calculated approximate visualization of the lightning structure. The representation is based on VLF data obtained from only three measuring stations placed on cars, so it contains many simplifications. For example, the lightning structure is depicted in a 2D plane positioned at a fixed height above the ground. Additionally, it is important to note that lightning discharges do not radiate at branching points, but primarily at the locations of two-thirds recoil leaders. The drawn connections do not reflect this fact, as the depiction is currently an illustrative algorithm that, despite its limitations, is a closer description of the physical principle of lightning discharges than the widely used depiction of points in a map.

\medskip

The algorithm used\urlnote{https://github.com/ODZ-UJF-AV-CR/CRREAT_cars/blob/master/VLF_lightning/VLF_location.ipynb}
for processing and visualizing VLF recordings from three measurement vehicles
operates as follows.

\seccc Signal preprocessing and segmentation

The recordings are first aligned according to absolute time marks derived from GNSS PPS triggers. 
Each raw signal $s_i(t)$ is normalized to zero mean and unit variance, and slow offsets are removed.
The prepared waveforms are divided into a number of contiguous segments $S_k$
chosen such that each segment contains comparable total energy $E_k$
determined relative to the weakest signal $E_k$:
$$
E_k = \int_{S_k} |s_i(t)|^2 \, dt , \qquad
E_k \approx E_{\rm min} \,.
\eqmark
$$

\seccc Cross-correlation and time-difference of arrival

For every segment, the cross-correlation function between the signals from
stations $i$ and $j$ is computed as
$$
R_{ij}(\tau) = \int s_i(t)\,s_j(t+\tau)\,dt \,,
\eqmark
$$
where $\tau$ is the time shift applied to the signal from station $j$ relative to station $i$.
The time offset corresponding to the correlation maximum is then
$$
\Delta t_{ij} = \mathop{\rm arg\,max}_{\tau} R_{ij}(\tau) \,.
\eqmark
$$

With one reference station 1, the delays reduce to
$$
\Delta t_i = t_i - t_1 \,.
\eqmark
$$

Physically impossible solutions are rejected using the condition
$$
|c \,\Delta t_i| < \Vert \vec r_i - \vec r_1 \Vert \,.
\eqmark
$$

\seccc Numerical localization

Assuming the radio wave propagates at the speed of light $c$, 
the distances between the source at position $\vec r=(x,y)$
and individual stations $\vec r_i$ satisfy
$$
\sqrt{(x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2} -
\sqrt{(x - x_1)^2 + (y - y_1)^2} = c\,\Delta t_i \,.
\eqmark
$$

The location $\hat{\vec r}$ is obtained by minimizing the sum of squared residuals,
$$
\hat{\vec r} = 
\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\vec r} \sum_i
\left(
\sqrt{(x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2} -
\sqrt{(x - x_1)^2 + (y - y_1)^2} -
c\,\Delta t_i
\right)^2 \,.
\eqmark
$$

In the presented simplified visualization, this full 3D solution
is projected onto a 2D plane at the expected cloud-base altitude.

\seccc Graph-based structural visualization

The final visualization stage converts the geometrical information an approximate channel-like structure. This is formulated as a graph construction
problem.

We denote by $\{\vec p_m\}$ a set of candidate spatial points. In practice, $\vec p_m$ are extrapolated continuation points along a bearing line with sufficient signal energy in the corresponding segment.

Formally, an intersection point $\vec p_{ij}$ between stations $i$ and $j$ is
obtained by solving
$$
\vec r_i + s_i\,\vec d_i = \vec r_j + s_j\,\vec d_j \,,
\eqmark
$$
for the parameters $s_i, s_j$.  
If a valid pair $(s_i,s_j)$ exists with $0 \le s_i,s_j \le L$,  
the common point
$$
\vec p_{ij} = \vec r_i + s_i\,\vec d_i
\eqmark
$$
is added to the set $\{\vec p_m\}$.

The visualization graph $G = (V,E)$ is then built as
$$
V = \{\vec p_m\},
\qquad
E = \{(\vec p_a,\vec p_b);\ 
\Vert \vec p_a - \vec p_b \Vert < d_{\rm max},\ 
|t_a - t_b| < \Delta t_{\rm max}\} \,.
\eqmark
$$

Here $d_{\rm max}$ is a spatial proximity threshold and $\Delta t_{\rm max}$
is a temporal adjacency threshold.  
The time $t_m$ assigned to point $\vec p_m$
corresponds to the time stamp of the signal segment from which that point was
derived.

Physically, the requirement $\Vert \vec p_a - \vec p_b \Vert < d_{\rm max}$ enforces
that only adjacent parts of a plausible lightning channel can be connected,
and $|t_a - t_b| < \Delta t_{\rm max}$ ensures causal ordering.

The final plotted structure is obtained by drawing all edges
$(\vec p_a,\vec p_b)\in E$ as short line segments:
$$
\Gamma = \bigcup_{(\vec p_a,\vec p_b)\in E}
\{\vec p_a + u(\vec p_b-\vec p_a);\ 0 \le u \le 1\} \,.
\eqmark
$$

\begitems
* It preserves topological continuity of the discharge, avoiding clustering steps typical for point-based mapping \cite[Sibolla2021].
* It suppresses spurious points: an isolated noisy point that has no compatible intersections will not generate edges in $E$ and will not appear in the final structure.
\enditems

Magnetic antennas, such as the STP antennas used in this research, do not measure the absolute value of the current 
$I(t)$ flowing through a lightning channel. Instead, they measure the time derivative of the magnetic field, 
${ dB \over dt }$. This fundamental limitation arises from Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, which governs the behavior of such antennas.

Faraday's law states that the induced electromotive force $\varepsilon$ in a closed loop is proportional to the time 
derivative of the magnetic flux $\Phi_B$ passing through the loop:

$$
\varepsilon = -{ d\Phi_B \over dt }.
\eqmark
$$

The magnetic flux $\Phi_B$ is defined as:

$$
\Phi_B = \int_S B \cdot dA,
\eqmark
$$

where:
\begitems
* $B$: Magnetic flux density (magnetic field),
* $S$: Surface enclosed by the loop,
* $dA$: Differential surface area vector, perpendicular to $S$.
\enditems

For a small loop antenna with a constant surface area $S$ and uniform magnetic field across its plane, the flux simplifies to:

$$
\Phi_B = B \cdot S,
\eqmark
$$

where $B$ is the magnetic field component perpendicular to the loop. Substituting into Faraday's law gives:

$$
\varepsilon = -S { dB \over dt }.
\eqmark
$$

Thus, the antenna measures the rate of change of the magnetic field, ${ dB \over dt }$, rather than the field $B$ itself.

The magnetic field $B(t)$ generated by a current $I(t)$ in a lightning channel at a distance $r$ is described by the Biot-Savart law. For a straight channel approximation:

$$
B(t) = {\mu_0 \over 2\pi} {I(t) \over r},
\eqmark
$$

where:
\begitems
* $\mu_0$: Permeability of free space,
* $r$: Distance from the current to the measurement point.
\enditems

Taking the time derivative of $B(t)$ relates it to the rate of change of the current:

$$
{ dB \over dt } = {\mu_0 \over 2\pi r} { dI \over dt }.
\eqmark
$$

The induced EMF in the antenna is therefore:

$$
\varepsilon = -S {\mu_0 \over 2\pi r} { dI \over dt }.
\eqmark
$$

This equation highlights that the antenna output is proportional to ${ dI \over dt }$, the rate of change of the current, and not the current $I(t)$ itself.


To estimate the lightning current $I(t)$, the measured EMF $\varepsilon$ must be integrated over time:

$$
I(t) = \int \left( { 2\pi r \over \mu_0 S } \varepsilon(t) \right) dt + I_0,
\eqmark
$$

where $I_0$ is the initial current at the start of the measurement. However, in practice, the value of $I_0$ is unknown. This means the absolute current cannot be determined from the antenna signal alone. Additionally, any noise in the measurement of $\varepsilon(t)$ is amplified during integration, further complicating the estimation. Consequently, the absolute value of the lightning current $I(t)$ cannot be measured directly without additional information about its baseline or slowly varying components. This situation is illustrated in Fig~\ref[2022.05.20.19.11.39.428680_reduced.mp4].

Lightning detection networks that rely solely on magnetic antennas cannot directly provide the absolute lightning currents. Such measurements would require additional sensing elements, most likely magnetometers, to complement the data and allow for a more complete reconstruction of lightning current profiles.

\sec[conclusions] Conclusions

The research has significantly revised the understanding of lightning phenomena. The apparatus, initially designed to observe events lasting a few hundred milliseconds, Section~\ref[literature_review], revealed that lightning events typically last at least twice as long, with spatial extents exceeding tens of kilometers. These findings demand a shift in observational methods, requiring recording devices with memory depths greater than three seconds and expansion of the lightning observation area by over 28 times from the previously assumed size.

This extended geometry also challenges vertical radiation theory, suggesting that electric fields and RREA likely radiate horizontally or obliquely. Thesis is related stationary detectors (e.g., GEODOS01 on Poledník) identified a new type of ionizing radiation event lasting tens of milliseconds \mycite[j5], distinct from known TGFs and TGEs.  This may explain why ionizing radiation could not be detected from below storm clouds using the described measuring vehicles, emphasizing the need for elevated or in situ atmospheric observations.

Electric field measurements using standard ground-based mills failed to correlate with lightning discharges, underscoring the importance of UAV-based EFM instruments like THUNDERMILL01 and optimized ionizing radiation detectors for in situ observations. Optical and radio data further demonstrated that lightning initiation spans several seconds, far beyond the return stroke phase, which is statistically minor (<10\% of discharges). A focus on understanding the remaining 90\% of lightning events is essential.

Experimental data also indicated that lightning initiation likely occurs far from visible discharge areas. While a potential initiation event was captured on video (appendix \ref[attached_files]), its analysis was limited by missing contextual data, highlighting the need for larger-scale, multi-instrument setups to explore causality between ionizing radiation and lightning.

Finally, the study revealed critical shortcomings in current lightning detection networks. Existing systems oversimplify lightning as clusters of impulses, misinterpret data as ground strikes, and lose essential information. Lightning detection networks relying solely on magnetic antennas cannot provide direct information about lightning currents. To overcome this limitation, these networks must be expanded to include additional sensors, such as magnetometers or cameras, to capture complementary data and allow for the reconstruction of lightning current profiles. The proposed approach provides a more accurate visual representation of lightning development, marking a significant step forward but requiring further refinement.

\secc Flash duration as a design–critical quantity

Selecting an adequate recording interval for lightning events is essential for instrument design. Although \cite[rakov_uman_2003] mentions a typical flash duration of about 300~ms, this number alone does not specify what fraction of all flashes fits within that interval. A large radio-based study reports a median of about 350~ms \cite[Lopez_Pineda_Montanya_Velde_Fabro_Romero_2017], which improves intuition, but our measurements did not confirm this value.

Based on in~situ mobile ground measurements with systems that I designed and operated, we showed that more than one half of recorded flashes have a duration longer than 0.5~s \mycite[j1]. Instrumentation designed for a shorter interval would therefore fail to capture at least one half of the events. This result is conservative, since the distribution may be affected by detector sensitivity and thresholding. Recent work from 2024 that revises flash-grouping algorithms in lightning detection networks indicates longer statistical flash durations when impulses are grouped more realistically \cite[Yang_AtmosRes_2024_107349]. 

\it Impact: \rm The result informs memory sizing, pre-trigger buffer depth, and trigger logic, ensuring that the earliest lightning phases that may be relevant to ionising-radiation emission are captured.

\secc Angular–sensitive EFM for simultaneous static and dynamic electric-field measurement

Despite extensive measurements of electric fields during thunderstorms \cite[Jones1993JGR,Marshall1995JGR], the charge distribution in a storm cloud remains insufficiently constrained; models indicate complex structures \cite[Brothers_Bruning_2018_JAS,Popova_Sokol_2022_AtmosRes]. Experience with a commercial EFM revealed limitations for dynamic measurements \mycite[j1]. Within CRREAT we therefore developed an angular-sensitive EFM that can sense both the quasi-static field and the rapidly varying component at the same time, improving directional information and local gradients. The concept and parameters were introduced at ATMOHED-2024, and measurements were published in \mycite[c4].

\it Impact:\rm\ Improved physical interpretation of local field structure and of the relation between charge regions and lightning; more precise triggering of co-located detectors such as optical and particle sensors.

\secc Radiation enhancements with durations of tens of milliseconds

With the GEODOS network we documented enhancements of ionising radiation with durations of tens of milliseconds \mycite[j5] (Received: 22 August 2023, Accepted: 27 February 2024). These observations fill a gap between known classes: TGF (microseconds to~$\sim$1~ms) \cite[Fishman_Science_1994,Tavani_PRL_2011,Celestin_Pasko_2012_JGR,Tavani_2013_NHESS,Shmatov_2015_PLA] and TGE (seconds to minutes) \cite[Torii_2009_GRL,Torii_2011_GRL,Chilingarian_Mkrtchyan_2012_PRD,Dwyer_2014_SSR,Cummer_2015_GRL,Wada_2021_JGR,Chilingarian_2019_SciRep,Yuasa_2020_PTEP,Diniz_2021_JGR,Kuriyama_2023_JGR]. The enhancements occur during periods with regional lightning activity, while the nearest localised discharges can be many kilometres away, suggesting a non-local mechanism or remote driver. An independent publication \cite[Ostgaard_Nature_2024] in 2024 reported signals of similar duration, supporting our findings.

\it Impact:\rm\ The finding motivates multi-level timing strategies for signal acquisition in particle detectors and raises questions about spatially inhomogeneous acceleration regions and their connection to lightning dynamics.

\secc Balloon measurements of ionising radiation in the atmosphere

To support CRREAT goals we extended the methodology for determining the position of the Regener–Pfotzer maximum with multiple detector types and with the new TF-ATMON telemetry \mycite[j2]. We then demonstrated latitude dependence of the maximum position using flights in Sweden during HEMERA~2019 and flights in Czechia \mycite[j3]. These measurements strengthen comparability of the method across platforms and campaigns.

\it Impact:\rm\ Strengthened reference data on cosmic-radiation–atmosphere interactions, with clear implications for the design and calibration of airborne dosimetry sensors used at commercial flight levels.

\sec Future Work

Future research should focus on in-situ measurements, where UAVs, particularly unmanned autogyros, appear ideal. Autogyros, with their rotating rotor providing stability in gusty weather and offering an optimal platform for integrating instruments, can facilitate the measurement of electric and magnetic fields as 3D-space vectors, including ionizing radiation detection. 

Deploying such devices in future studies enables direct detection of RREA and reveals the directional structure of thunderstorm ionizing radiation sources. This knowledge is essential for interpreting ionizing radiation and lightning event data to explain lightning initiation and potentially predict lightning occurrence.

The TF-G2 autogyro has demonstrated the potential and challenges required by UAV technology for atmospheric research. Enhancing this technology will improve the ability to collect atmospheric data near or within storms, improve understanding of thunderstorm dynamics, improve weather prediction models, and contribute to climate studies. Therefore, continued development and field testing of autogyros and related technologies are necessary.
