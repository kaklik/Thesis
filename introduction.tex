\sec[objectives] Objectives and their Context

From a top-level perspective, the overall goal of this thesis is to advance the understanding of lightning phenomena and associated atmospheric radiation. Current understanding of lightning phenomena, particularly its spatial and temporal dynamics and its relationship with high-energy atmospheric radiation, is based on observational methods that may oversimplify its complexity. Existing Lightning Mapping Array networks often reduce lightning to localized impulse clusters \cite[11214545], potentially missing information about its full extent, phases, and energy transfer mechanisms. A more detailed, high-resolution, and multi-instrument approach is required to unravel these complexities, leading to improved lightning forecasting, safety protocols, and a deeper comprehension of atmospheric electricity.

The objectives of this dissertation adopt a physical, episode-based definition of a lightning event (first detected activity to last detected activity across modalities), rather than an algorithmic GLM (Geostationary Lightning Mapper) or LIS (Lightning Imaging Sensor) cluster definition. This directly addresses the underestimation bias. Therefore, helping to clarify unresolved phenomena related to thunderstorms, particularly the initiation and development of lightning, is reviewed in Section~\ref[problem_definition]. 

While technical standards, such as IEC 62305\cite[IEC62305-1_2024, IEC62305-2_2024, IEC62305-3_2024, IEC62305-4_2024], have been developed based on existing observations, their focus is on defining parameters for protecting technical devices against lightning currents. These standards simplify\cite[Berger2006_IEC62305_Params, Cai2022_TriggeredVsNatural, Rousseau2016_IEC62305_Limits] the modeling of lightning currents to address the needs of anthropogenic terrestrial objects, which are inherently influenced by artificial factors. In contrast, the scope of this dissertation is firmly rooted in fundamental research, aiming to study lightning discharges in their natural state. The focus is on understanding the intrinsic properties of lightning phenomena without interference from man-made structures.

This research aims to utilize new tools for the scientific observation of lightning events within thunderstorms. The focus will be on understanding the relationship between ionizing radiation and lightning. The specific tasks to be undertaken are as follows:

\begitems \style n
* {\bf Implementation of Lightning Detection Apparatus}: Design and deploy a mobile experimental apparatus capable of scientific-grade detection and localization of lightning events and ionizing radiation.
* {\bf Enhance understanding of Spatial and Temporal Characteristics of Lightning Discharges}: Enhance visualization methods and compare observations across different radiation domains to achieve a better understanding of the spatial and temporal development of lightning.
* {\bf Enhancement of Measurement Techniques with in-situ atmospheric monitoring}: Integrate new instruments into aerial vehicles for in-situ atmospheric measurements.
\enditems

The results of this research are expected to provide a deeper understanding of lightning initiation and propagation. At the same time, it offers improved detection and observation techniques that can inform future studies about the relation to ionizing radiation with direct practical applications in meteorology and atmospheric physics.

These three objectives define the scope of this dissertation and are evaluated in Section~\ref[fulfilment] and Section~\ref[contributions].

\sec[problem_definition] Problem Statement

The field of lightning research continues to face significant challenges, particularly due to the lack of unified theoretical and experimental frameworks describing lightning initiation, propagation, and interaction with man-made structures. Existing technical standards and methodologies often diverge, reflecting the complexity of atmospheric electrical processes and the absence of a universally accepted model of lightning behavior. This diversity of approaches underscores the need for further systematic research in the field.

Studying lightning is inherently difficult. Most current experimental methods rely on technical approximations (such as laboratory impulse current generators or rocket-triggered lightning experiments) that provide valuable, yet incomplete, representations of natural discharges. Similarly, measurements conducted on tall structures like communication or meteorological towers often record events whose parameters differ from those of free-field natural lightning. These discrepancies emphasize the importance of in situ observations designed to capture the genuine characteristics and variability of natural lightning phenomena under real atmospheric conditions.

Building on this understanding, the following scientific assumptions, derived from the literature review, form the conceptual foundation of this research. Although they reflect the prevailing knowledge in the field, this dissertation seeks to examine and verify them through empirical analysis, thereby contributing to a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of thunderstorm and lightning processes.

\begitems
* Research on high-energy atmospheric phenomena in thunderstorms has traditionally emphasized the strongest local electric fields present during lightning initiation, where relativistic runaway electron avalanches are believed to develop and produce bursts of ionizing radiation. \cite[Dwyer2003, DwyerUman2014, Gurevich_Milikh_Roussel-Dupre_1992]
* Lightning is often treated as a transient phenomenon consisting of brief, impulsive processes. Individual return strokes in negative cloud-to-ground flashes exhibit sub- to few-hundred–microsecond-scale current surges and associated electromagnetic radiation\cite[Uman1969, rakov_uman_2003]. In many operational and remote-sensing frameworks, a lightning event is further assumed to last no more than a few hundred milliseconds, typically 300 ms, before it is considered to have ended. \cite[rakov_uman_2003]
* Classical models and engineering standards typically assume that an individual lightning discharge occupies a limited spatial region on the order of only a few to several tens of kilometers in horizontal extent. \cite[Thottappillil1992, rakov_uman_2003] This view treats lightning as a relatively localized source rather than a storm-scale structure.
* Bursts and enhancements of ionizing radiation in thunderstorms (including terrestrial gamma-ray flashes (TGFs) and ground-level gamma-ray enhancements) have been observed in close temporal association with lightning leader development and strong local electric fields\cite[Dwyer2012, Photonuclear_reactions_triggered].
These emissions are often interpreted as bremsstrahlung from relativistic electrons accelerated in the high-field regions of developing lightning channels.
* Historically, lightning research has emphasized the high-current return stroke of negative cloud-to-ground flashes, which produces the dominant electromagnetic pulse and is directly relevant to lightning protection engineering. \cite[Uman1969, Berger1975,rakov_uman_2003] This return-stroke–centric view underlies most classical models of lightning current and many lightning protection standards.
\enditems

Besides mapping lightning using TDoA, several attempts have been made to map lightning using radio interferometry \cite[Purier2020NewAT, Chilingarian_interferometer, rs15071923]. The InLMA system \cite[rs15071923] has been even utilizing multiple interferometric stations simultaneously.

Unfortunatelly the existing lightning observation systems are often geographically fixed \cite[Thomas2001_GRL_LMA_VHF, Chmielewski2016_JTECH_LMA_Performance, WWLLN2018_Overview] or lack the comprehensive suite of sensors needed to capture both the optical and electromagnetic signatures of lightning simultaneously with associated ionzing radiation\cite[natural_high-energy, Siingh_cosmic_rays, Xray_enhancements, McCarthy1985-tc, Xray_electric_field,gamma-ray_dose_increase, Ground-based_observations_thunderstorm].
It is assumed that the source of this radiation is bremsstrahlung generated by electrons accelerated by an electric field in the thunderclouds \cite[gamma-ray_dose_increase, Ground-based_observations_thunderstorm, Photonuclear_reactions_triggered, Termination_electron_acceleration, Ebro_delta_region]. These electrons that are accelerated to relativistic velocities are called relativistic runaway electron avalanches (RREAs), which then interact with the atmosphere \cite[Dwyer_2003, Gurevich_Milikh_Roussel-Dupre_1992]. This causes a phenomenon that is often called \ii terrestrial/gamma-ray/flash/(TGF) terrestrial gamma-ray flash (TGF) \cite[Fishman_Bhat_Mallozzi_Horack_Koshut_Kouveliotou_Pendleton_Meegan_Wilson_Paciesas_et al._1994] or another phenomenon like \ii thunderstorm/ground/enhancement/(TGE) thunderstorm ground enhancement (TGE) \cite[Chilingarian_2013, Torii_Takeishi_Hosono_2002]. For both, the source of the radiation is thought to be somewhat associated with the RREA, although it has not been experimentally proven; the main difference is the time span in which they occur \cite[Dwyer_2003, Gurevich_Milikh_Roussel-Dupre_1992]. Moreover, the TGFs were only rarely successfully measured at the ground level \cite[2021JD036130, 2012JA017810, 123061103].

Furthermore, recent experimental results show that there is evidence of other ionizing radiation manifestations generated by the thunderstorms. For example, there has been experimental evidence of the interaction of high-energy photons with the atmosphere causing nuclear reactions \cite[Enoto_Wada_Furuta_Nakazawa_Yuasa_Okuda_Makishima_Sato_Sato_Nakano_et al._2017].

The ionizing radiation that is thought to be associated with storm activity was measured using satellites in orbit around the Earth \cite[Østgaard_Neubert_Reglero_Ullaland_Yang_Genov_Marisaldi_Mezentsev_Kochkin_Lehtinen_et al._2019], aircrafts flying inside or in the vicinity of storm clouds \cite[Kochkin_van Deursen_Marisaldi_Ursi_de Boer_Bardet_Allasia_Boissin_Flourens_Østgaard_2017, McCarthy_Parks_1985, Parks_Mauk_Spiger_Chin_1981] or high mountain observatories e.g. \cite[Chilingarian_Hovsepyan_Hovhannisyan_2011, Chum_Langer_Baše_Kollárik_Strhárský_Diendorfer_Rusz_2020, Tsuchiya_Enoto_Torii_Nakazawa_Yuasa_Torii_Fukuyama_Yamaguchi_Kato_Okano_et al._2009]. Currently, there are only a few measurements that could confirm the existence of ionizing radiation at lower altitudes, except for special storms that occur during the winter season in Japan, where storm clouds emerge low above the ground \cite[Michimoto_2007].
That experiment includes the mapping of a radio signal emitted by lightning \cite[Rison_Thomas_Krehbiel_Hamlin_Harlin_1999, Wu_Wang_Takagi_2018, Abbasi_Abu-Zayyad_Allen_Barcikowski_Belz_Bergman_Blake_Byrne_Cady_Cheon_et al._2018]. Despite these very detailed observations, the exact location of the source of the ionizing radiation emergence as a result of electric fields within the storm cloud remains unknown \cite[2019JD031940]. 

For \ii cloud-to-ground/flash cloud-to-ground (CG) flashes, the structure typically includes a sudden start with a stepped leader, in contrast to \ii cloud-to-cloud/flash cloud-to-cloud (CC) flashes that initially showcase a slower train of noise pulses. \cite[LeVine1978TheTS].
While the general mechanics of CG lightning—its initiation and propagation—have been linked to specific atmospheric conditions such as the presence of graupel, ice, and hail, the differential occurrence rates and damaging potential of positive versus negative discharges, still invite further investigation \cite[ribaud:hal-01386421].

Intracloud (IC) lightning discharges should have characteristic radio pulses, which consist of a uniform burst pattern. These bursts are described as a distinctive waveform characterized by a fast, large amplitude pulse followed by a smaller, slowly varying overshoot\cite[https://doi.org/10.1029/JC080i027p03801].

The \ii compact/intracloud/discharge Compact Intracloud lightning Discharge (CID), a particular type of intracloud lightning, is described as a bouncing-wave phenomenon. This involves multiple reflections occurring at both ends of the radiating channel, contributing to its fine structure and accompanying high-frequency (HF) and very high-frequency (VHF) radiation bursts \cite[5184875]. The strongest pulses should typically occur in isolation or at the beginning of leader progression. These pulses are sometimes associated with rapid electric charge relaxation and are not necessarily accompanied by visible light emissions. In instances where these pulses initiate, they are followed by an upward-progressing leader \cite[https://doi.org/10.1029/2003JD003936].

The study of lightning-induced \ii radio/pulses radio pulses has been also expanded by observations from the satellites, which identify additional characteristics in these emissions. Some exhibit steep roll-offs of power within certain frequency ranges, while others demonstrate flat-spectrum behavior. This distinction indicates the somewhat varied nature of lightning’s electromagnetic emissions \cite[https://doi.org/10.1029/1998RS900043].


%\sec[lightning_signals] Key radio signatures of lightning events

%\secc K-changes

%\iid K-changes, or K-complexes, refer to a specific pattern of rapid waveform of electric field change observed. The term K-change is used today to denote relatively small step-like static field changes that occur between and after return strokes, and also during intra-cloud flashes \cite[Nanayakkara]. A \ii return/stroke return stroke is the high-current electric discharge in lightning, occurring when the downward leader connects with an upward streamer, rapidly neutralizing the charge between ground and cloud and creating a bright, intense flash. This process involves currents typically reaching tens of thousands of amperes, propagating upwards at one-third the speed of light \cite[rakov_uman_2003].

%\secc Narrow Bipolar Events (NBEs)

%NBEs are distinct, intense radio pulses with a very short duration, typically a few microseconds, and are considered the most powerful natural VHF (Very High Frequency) sources in the Earth's atmosphere. They exhibit a remarkably narrow bipolar pulse shape and are believed to result from a rapid discharge process within thunderclouds, possibly associated with the initiation stages of lightning \cite[Wu2012DischargeHO].

%\secc Sferics

%\iid Sferics is short for atmospherics, the term used for radio waves emitted by lightning discharges. These signals, spanning a broad range of frequencies but most commonly observed in VLF and LF (Low Frequency) bands, represent the electromagnetic signature of distant lightning's return stroke. Sferics carry distinctive timing information, making them valuable for long-range lightning detection and location systems \cite[Koochak].

Their temporal evolution demonstrates self-similarity across different time scales, reflecting the complex and dynamic processes underlying lightning phenomena.

\sec[literature_review] Spatiotemporal Scales of Lightning Discharges
Understanding the characteristic spatial extent and temporal duration of lightning discharges is foundational for multiple adjacent research threads, including atmospheric ionizing radiation, lightning initiation physics, and hazard modeling. Yet, despite decades of study, the field still lacks consensus statistics (e.g., medians and interquartile ranges) for whole-flash size and duration. Instead, two threads dominate the literature: (i) early RF/field-change studies that characterized intra-flash structure qualitatively, and (ii) modern satellite optical measurements that excel at mapping extremes but whose flash-clustering algorithms complicate population-level timing and size metrics.

The mechanisms triggering lightning and the potential involvement of high-energy phenomena like relativistic runaway electron avalanches (RREA) are still not fully understood. 

Atmospheric electromagnetic phenomena have been identified as mandatory physical properties influencing atmospheric radiation processes. These phenomena, including lightning, sprites, and other events, are also a significant part of the formation of cloud structures and significantly impact the movement and characteristics of atmospherically relevant subsystems \cite[npg-20-293-2013].

The evolution of radio transmission has been one of the earliest methods of regular observation of atmospheric electricity (e.g. sferics). It has rapidly revealed the existence of atmospheric charge structures, such as the ionosphere and its variations, which form the atmospheric waveguide allowing the propagation of radio waves produced by lightning over long distances. Research has also delved into the dynamics of geomagnetic pulsation regimes and their interaction with the Earth's magnetosphere, reflecting the interactions resulting from powerful solar flares \cite[Parkhomov2021GeomagneticPB].

In parallel, ongoing meteorological research, the integration of advanced technologies from aviation, starting from balloons, led to the discovery of cosmic radiation \cite[Hess:1912srp, REGENER1935]. The balloon observations were combined with radio transmission to be automated in the form of radiosondes \cite[8561fc97-ffa1-39dd-ae09-da46857c3d48, https://doi.org/10.1029/2006JD008187]. That technology has been improved and used for decades, until the most recent stage of  evolution in  Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) \cite[CHANG2020126867].

A significant contribution of radio engineering in meteorology comes from the invention of radars, which are used for remote sensing of atmospheric structures.  Remote radio sensing and in-situ measuring methods are also combined to obtain multiple perspective views on electric phenomena \cite[Fabry_2015, https://doi.org/10.1029/2018JA025622, bb278c67c86b4412b264b47fd73da169].

The increasing requirements on lightning safety during the Space Race significantly enhanced the technology of ground-based equipment such as electric field mills and radio-based lightning detection and mapping systems \cite[LeVine1978TheTS, Amman1970, 5173582]. The state of art of this radio-based technology utilizes large area EFM arrays for observation in the form of non-intrusive methods to determine atmospheric electric fields and also mapping of lightning evolution and structure. Notable examples of these systems are New Mexico Tech's Lightning Mapping Array (LMA) \cite[https://doi.org/10.1002/2016JD025159],  FALMA \cite[https://doi.org/10.1002/2018GL077628], community-based network Blitzortung.org \cite[Blitzortung.org] and also LOFAR radio telescope  \cite[https://doi.org/10.1002/2017JD028132].

\secc RF and E-field measurements

As example instruments, there could be mentioned the Vaisala lightning sensors \cite[vaisala],  WWLLN \cite[https://doi.org/10.1029/2021RS007293], and LINET \cite[Betz2009].

Le Vine’s NASA Technical Memorandum~\cite[LeVine1978_NASA_TM] reported that lightning RF emission (3–300 MHz) appears as trains of discrete pulses whose time histories differ systematically between cloud-to-ground and intracloud flashes; “mixed” cases reflect breakdown phases preceding stepped leaders. The work emphasized frequency-independent patterns across the monitored band and highlighted the feasibility of studying flash duration and intra-flash pulse statistics, but did not deliver population-scale timing distributions.

While the NASA Technical Memorandum by Le Vine remains a foundational, phenomenological description of RF emissions from lightning, it is also a 1970s-era study and should be interpreted accordingly.

\secc Satellite-based optical mapping 

Modern satellite imagers such as the Optical Transient Detector (OTD), Lightning Imaging Sensor (LIS), 
and the Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM) aboard the GOES-R series have revolutionized global lightning observations 
by providing continuous, hemispheric coverage at millisecond-scale temporal resolution 
\cite[Boccippio2000_OTD_LIS,Christian2003_LIS_OTD,Goodman2013_GLM_ProcIEEE].  

However, what the instrument reports as a ``flash'' is not a direct physical quantity but the result of ground-based clustering logic.  
For GLM, a flash is defined as a set of optical groups separated by no more than 330~ms in time and 16.5~km in space; 
if either threshold is exceeded, the event is split into multiple flashes 
\cite[Goodman2013_GLM_ProcIEEE,Peterson2021_JGR_GLMClustering].  
These thresholds are engineering parameters optimized for onboard processing rather than physical boundaries of a single electrical discharge.
Similar hierarchical grouping is implemented in the LIS and OTD data pipelines 
\cite[Boccippio2002_OTD_Clustering].

Initial GLM climatologies reported large-scale tendencies, such as longer and larger flashes over ocean compared to land, 
with typical mean durations of a few hundred milliseconds depending on the convective regime 
\cite[Peterson2020_JGR_GLMClimatology,Rudlosky2019_BAMS_GLMPerf].  
More recent assessments confirm that GLM-derived flash durations and energetics are highly sensitive to 
storm morphology, satellite viewing geometry, background brightness, and algorithmic tuning 
\cite[Peterson2021_JGR_GLMClustering,Albrecht2020_JGR_GLMValidation].  

Despite these remarkable advances, the phenomena described in Section~\ref[problem_definition] remain only partially explained.
In particular, the relationships among optical emissions, electromagnetic waveforms, and associated high-energy radiation 
still lack a consistent physical interpretation \cite[Boccippio2000_OTD_LIS,Christian2003_LIS_OTD,Goodman2013_GLM_ProcIEEE,Peterson2021_JGR_GLMClustering,Boccippio2002_OTD_Clustering,Peterson2020_JGR_GLMClimatology,Rudlosky2019_BAMS_GLMPerf,Albrecht2020_JGR_GLMValidation].




\secc[KnownRecords] Known records without statistics

Spaceborne imagers have established remarkable ``megaflash'' records that push the known limits of flash extent and duration. 
The current \it length \rm record is $829$\,km (22~Oct~2017, U.S. Great Plains; reported optical activity $\sim7.4$\,s), which superseded the previous $768\pm8$\,km record from 29~Apr~2020 over the southern United States. 
The \it duration \rm record stands at $17.1$\,s (18~Jun~2020, Uruguay–Argentina) \cite[WMO2025_Megaflash,WMO2022_Megaflash]. 
These events demonstrate what geostationary optical sensors can observe over vast mesoscale convective systems and are invaluable for bounding physical plausibility and potential hazards.

However, extremes are not design points. 
Engineering of instruments for lightning–radiation studies requires population information, central tendencies (e.g., medians of duration/area/length) and robust dispersion metrics, rather than the rarest realizations. 
Focusing on the absolute maxima would lead to inefficient designs optimized for conditions that occur infrequently (and whose occurrence rate is not well constrained).

A compounding difficulty is methodological: satellite optical ``flashes'' are products of ground clustering of optical groups (e.g., GLM uses $\le 330$\,ms and $\le 16.5$\,km thresholds), so reported durations and lengths are sensitive to detection efficiency, viewing geometry, background, and algorithmic tuning \cite[Peterson2021_JGR_GLMClustering,Peterson2020_JGR_GLMClimatology,Rudlosky2019_BAMS_GLMPerf,Boccippio2002_OTD_Clustering]. 
Consequently, widely cited statements such as “typical flashes last $\sim300$\,ms” often reflect algorithmic timeouts rather than the full duration of the underlying electrical episode. 
Systematic, population-level statistics that are physically defined (rather than processing-defined) remain sparse.

A key objective of this dissertation is therefore to quantify population distributions of physically meaningful episode lengths, so that instrument design can target the typical operating envelope with quantified margins.



\secc[ArtificialLightning] Artificially generated and structure-induced lightning

A large fraction of waveform data historically used for lightning-current standardization and protection design originates from 
measurements on anthropogenic objects or from artificially triggered discharges. 
Although such experiments are invaluable for quantifying current magnitudes and validating protection systems, 
they do not represent the natural, free-air development of lightning channels. 
In particular, the standardized current waveforms in IEC~62305 are simplified test shapes for engineering purposes 
and should not be interpreted as faithful templates of natural lightning in unperturbed conditions 
\cite[Berger2006_IEC62305_Params,Rousseau2016_IEC62305_Limits].

Triggered-lightning campaigns, initiated by launching rockets with grounded wires into charged storm regions,
have enabled controlled recording of current waveforms and electromagnetic fields 
\cite[Qie2024_TriggeredLightning,Li2019_UndergroundMagPulses,ArtificialTriggered2012_Study].  
However, the resulting discharges propagate along pre-ionized, low-impedance channels that fundamentally alter
the leader development, current rise rate, and optical signature compared with spontaneous lightning.
Typical return-stroke currents of triggered flashes (10--20~kA) and their normalized waveforms 
differ from many natural cloud-to-ground (CG) events, which can exceed 100~kA and exhibit complex multi-stroke structures. 
Therefore, datasets dominated by triggered discharges are not representative of the natural free-air waveform families 
and are misaligned with the physical scope assumed for IEC~62305 test shapes 
\cite[Cai2022_TriggeredVsNatural,Berger2006_IEC62305_Params].

Similarly, measurements obtained from instrumented towers, wind turbines, or skyscrapers 
describe a distinct lightning regime --- upward lightning --- in which the discharge initiates 
from the structure itself rather than descending from the cloud.
These events are strongly influenced by the geometry, height, and electrical environment of the object, 
and they often feature continuous current components and prolonged, low-amplitude leaders.  
Consequently, upward-lightning waveforms measured on man-made structures should not be conflated with 
natural CG/IC waveforms, nor with the simplified test waveforms used in IEC~62305 for equipment protection, 
which deliberately abstract from such site-specific effects 
\cite[Berger2006_IEC62305_Params,Rousseau2016_IEC62305_Limits].

In summary, both triggered-lightning and upward-lightning datasets provide critical insights for engineering applications 
but offer limited validity for studies of the intrinsic physical processes of natural lightning.  
The present dissertation therefore focuses on field measurements conducted away from tall structures and without artificial triggering, 
to capture the natural temporal and energetic evolution of lightning discharges.


\sec Research Methodology and Planned Approaches

Based on the theoretical framework and assumptions outlined in Section~\ref[literature_review], I proposed the research methodology. This methodology includes the use of measuring vehicles and unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to improve the detection of radiation phenomena associated with thunderstorms. The primary objective is to position detection devices as close to or beneath the storm core as possible, thereby increasing the likelihood of capturing relevant radiation events. That approach is based on the following key components:

\begitems
* {\bf Utilization of Ground Measuring Vehicles:}
I use measuring vehicles equipped with specialized instruments for detecting radio signals and ionizing radiation. These vehicles also serve as mobile platforms, providing the necessary ground support for the UAVs. They are at the same time equipped with a range of auxiliary devices to capture the interrelationships between the measured data. For example, scintillation detectors for ionizing radiation must account for the influence of temperature changes on sensitivity, and disdrometers are used to measure the intensity and type of precipitation that can wash radon products from the atmosphere, causing increased radiation levels, which could easily be inappropriately interpreted as TGE.
* {\bf Deployment of UAVs for Radiation and Electric Field Detection:}
Multiple UAVs are equipped with electric field mills (EFMs) and detectors for ionizing radiation and are tasked with locating and identifying sources of ionizing radiation in the atmosphere. This method leverages the presumption that the intensity of generated ionizing radiation diminishes with increasing distance from its source. Therefore, UAVs could provide data on ionizing radiation that is difficult to obtain from ground-level measurements. UAVs are supposed to observe thunderstorms cooperatively with measuring cars.
\enditems

The flowchart depicted in Fig. \ref[workflow] illustrates a high-level workflow of the research. It begins with the setup of measurement cars equipped with lightning detection and recording instruments. Experimental measurements are then conducted under thunderstorm clouds, then the recorded lightning events are analyzed for the presence of ionizing radiation. Parallel to this, the UAV autogyro and other instruments are designed to aid in these measurements. Observations are then supposed to be made from higher altitudes or side angles to enhance data collection. Then the data from the measurement cars and UAVs needs to be combined.

\sec Outline of the thesis

The initial work of the thesis begins with chapter~\ref[triggered_recording], which describes the construction and test of a device engineered to automatically produce trigger signals based on lightning events. The chapter has been one of the initial work packages because the described methods enable the recording of instruments synchronized with the lightning. That is the essential feature needed for every additional step of the following research.

This is attributed to the fact that within the scope of storm evolution, lightning can be seen as occurring in relatively brief and sparse periods compared to the more extended spans of storm activity without lightning (and supposedly also without ionizing radiation), as discussed in Sections~\ref[literature_review] and \ref[problem_definition]. Simultaneously, the flow of individual ionizing radiation particles or photons during TGF or TGE spans a broad spectrum of possible energy levels. That does not permit the setting of an exact recording threshold at the individual instrument, given the sensitivity capabilities of available equipment. Therefore, ionizing radiation detectors, and the majority of other instruments, like radio receivers and high-speed cameras, are required to capture event data throughout its actual occurrence, and this data fragment should subsequently be analyzed to examine the underlying phenomena.

Therefore, these instruments are at the same time examples of scientific devices capable of generating an enormous amount of data. Such volumes of data are (with the actual technology level) difficult to store within the intervals between events, e.g., lightning. That is the main rationale behind the need to create a reliable method of triggering the recording of all instruments, depending on lightning activity, to store event-based data fragments.

Chapter \ref[instrumentation] explored the exact design of the innovative tools and devices engineered especially to improve the study of lightning in thunderstorms and the related atmospheric phenomena. The design and development of these instruments were initially guided by literature listed in Chapter~\ref[literature_review], which suggested typical lightning properties that early experiments soon called into question. This suspicion led to multiple iterations in the construction of instrumentation, as the original assumption of lightning's brevity—thought to correlate the event data with the vertical development of lightning channels over tens of milliseconds—proved grossly underestimated, as the actual ranges of these events are several orders of magnitude greater. This situation also changes the requirement on examined electric field gradients, because the vertical gradient detection needs to be exchanged by a horizontal or more ideally, the 3D space distribution.  The chapter discusses the rationale behind the instrument development, its design principles, and how these advancements improve upon existing technologies. It also details the experimental setup and integration of these tools into broader research methodology, emphasizing their future potential to significantly advance the understanding of atmospheric electricity.

However, because it was necessary to first verify the developed instruments on more deterministic processes than direct measuring during storm activity, a large number of supportive experiments were conducted, which served to verify the properties of the instruments and their supporting subsystems. These experiments included flights of stratospheric balloons and UAVs, as well as ground measurements with similar instrumentation, either at stationary or mobile measuring stations. Chapter~\ref[experiments] therefore documents the extensive preparatory work necessary to validate the proposed instruments' efficacy before their direct application in thunderstorm research. Chapter~\ref[results] synthesizes the insights gained from the application of the developed instruments in the field, reflecting on the contributions this research has made to the field of atmospheric sciences, particularly in understanding lightning phenomena.
